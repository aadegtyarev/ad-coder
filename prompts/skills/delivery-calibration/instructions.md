Know what the work will cost before spending it, and say so in terms the operator can answer.

Before dispatch, state: the task shape, its complexity, the surfaces in scope, the cost range from comparable accepted work, finite budgets, the acceptance gates, and the stop condition. A dispatch without a stop condition runs until a limit ends it, and a limit is a worse decision-maker than you are.

## Answering a budget

When the operator names a figure, answer with one of three things and never with silence:

- **Accept** — the estimate fits, and you say what it is based on.
- **Counter with evidence** — "work of this shape has cost about $15; $10 may not fit", naming the comparable work. A counter without evidence is a hunch wearing a number.
- **Admit you cannot estimate** — say so, and say what a bounded look at the project would cost to find out. Estimating blind is not a virtue, and a confident number invented from nothing is worse than no number.

Ground an estimate in recorded outcomes where any exist. Where none exist, say that plainly rather than producing a figure that sounds researched.

## While spending it

Optimize accepted-result cost, not token count. Planning, research, repair, re-review, failed stages and recovery all belong to the route being evaluated — a cheap model that needs two rounds of rework was not cheap.

On a subscription the comparable quantity is the fraction of that model's allowance, not the dollars: two models at the same list price can differ fourfold in what the subscription actually grants.

## When a stage runs out

Three different events wear that one symptom, and they need different answers:

- **Underestimate** — progress is real and the scope is closing. Raise it, record the correction so the next run starts from the better number.
- **Loop** — turns continue and progress does not: the same tools on the same targets, nothing new. Stop and report what you observed. A raised budget here buys more circling.
- **Too large for this model** — progress is real but the scope keeps widening rather than closing. Decompose and dispatch the slices; a smaller piece may fit the cheaper model already routed to the cell, which is better than escalating the whole task.

Report a diagnosis, not a question. "The stage ran out, raise it?" gives the operator nothing to decide — the only answer is yes, and you know more than they do at that moment. "This role on this model is not progressing: nine turns, the same three files, no new tool targets" is something they can act on.

Treat one failure as evidence about this task shape, not a global demotion of the model. And treat your own routing as a calibration sample: compare what you chose against rounds to acceptance, limit hits, rework and accepted-result cost, and persist a project-local correction when they disagree repeatedly — never rewrite the reusable baseline from one run.
